Business plan: Automated 3D visualization

This proposal follows the intrapreneur guidelines. The initial pilot would use existing company resources, while tracking incremental computing costs and development effort.

Business opportunity

Companies with existing 3D assets often spend time and money producing consistent images for catalogs, sales proposals, and product variations. The opportunity is to automate this workflow rather than simply rent computing power.

The initial target would be furniture and product manufacturers that need recurring batches of images from existing glTF or GLB models. The service would apply predefined camera, lighting, and material settings to generate consistent views and product variants.

Proposed solution

The existing C++ renderer loads glTF and GLB scenes, supports textures, cameras, shadows, direct lighting, diffuse indirect bounces, BVH acceleration, progressive tile rendering, and headless PNG output.

A prototype workflow would accept a model and rendering template, validate the input, generate images, and return the results. A simple command-line interface is sufficient for the initial pilot; a web interface and API can follow if demand is demonstrated.

Competitive advantage

Cloud rendering services and established 3D tools already provide rendering capacity. The proposed differentiation is a simpler, repeatable workflow: model validation, reusable templates, batch generation, consistent output, and integration with customer processes.

Parallel execution could reduce turnaround time when processing many independent rendering jobs. Its value must be demonstrated through benchmarks against suitable alternatives, including established renderers. C++ implementation alone does not guarantee a performance advantage.

Initial scope and limitations

The pilot would support a deliberately narrow workflow and a documented set of glTF/GLB features. Customers would need suitable, render-ready assets.

The current shading model is primarily diffuse and does not yet provide complete physically based rendering. Photorealistic product imagery may therefore require substantial improvements to reflective materials, normal mapping, environment lighting, tone mapping, and sampling.

Before investing in these features, the prototype should be tested against representative customer assets and compared with an established renderer. If visual quality is insufficient, a simpler technical-visualization use case may be a better starting point.

Business model

The first offering would be a paid pilot combining template setup and automated image generation. This would test willingness to pay while revealing how much manual intervention each job requires.

If the workflow proves repeatable, possible next steps include monthly subscriptions, API-based pricing, and private deployments for customers with strict confidentiality requirements.

Validation roadmap

  1. Validate demand: interview prospective customers about their current workflow, costs, output requirements, and existing alternatives.
  2. Demonstrate feasibility: process representative models and compare image quality, rendering time, and setup effort against established tools.
  3. Run a paid pilot: deliver a real batch of images and measure customer satisfaction, repeat usage, and willingness to pay.
  4. Productize: improve error handling, testing, logging, configuration, and input validation based on pilot results.
  5. Scale selectively: introduce job queues, APIs, persistent storage, and additional workers only when demonstrated demand justifies them.

Costs and success criteria

Track computing costs, development effort, model preparation time, storage, failed jobs, retries, and support. Estimate contribution margin by subtracting variable delivery costs from revenue.

The pilot succeeds if customers receive useful images reliably, the workflow reduces their effort or turnaround time, and at least one customer is willing to pay for the result.

Conclusion

The project is a promising technical foundation, not yet a validated business. Its potential lies in turning existing 3D assets into useful visual content through a convenient and repeatable workflow.

The recommended next step is a small, measurable pilot. Investment in rendering improvements and service infrastructure should follow evidence of customer demand, acceptable visual quality, and viable delivery costs.
