I've just read an article about how AI providers charge for usage. Here's what I understood.

## How it works

When you send a prompt to an AI model, the provider charges you based on the number of tokens processed.

There are three main costs:

- Input tokens: the tokens sent to the model.
- Output tokens: the tokens generated by the model.
- Cached tokens: previously processed input tokens that can be reused at a reduced price.

Prices are usually expressed in dollars per million tokens (MT).

Let's define the following variables:

- \(I\): input price (\$/MT).
- \(O\): output price (\$/MT).
- \(W\): cache write price (\$/MT).
- \(R\): cache read price (\$/MT).
- \(X\): input length (MT).
- \(Y\): output length (MT).

Without caching, the cost of a single request is:

$$
C = X \cdot I + Y \cdot O
$$

Notice that the model's maximum context length is not necessarily the number of tokens billed. You pay for the tokens
actually processed, according to the provider's pricing rules.

What happens when you send a second prompt? Will you pay \(X \cdot I\) again for the input?

Not necessarily! Some providers offer **prompt caching**, which allows previously processed input tokens to be reused at
a lower price.

There are two important operations:

- **Cache write:** Storing input tokens for potential reuse. This may cost more than regular input processing.
- **Cache read (cache hit):** Reusing cached tokens at a reduced price.

A cache read is possible only if the relevant content has already been cached and the cache is still valid.

Think of caching as temporarily storing a reusable portion of your prompt. The provider keeps that content available for
a limited time, potentially reducing the cost of subsequent requests.

For example, a provider might offer a five-minute cache lifetime. The exact duration and pricing depend on the provider
and model.

## Example

Suppose we make 15 requests, each processing 30,000 input tokens and generating an average of 1,500 output tokens.

Assume the following prices:

- \(I = \$2/\text{MT}\)
- \(O = \$10/\text{MT}\)
- \(X = 30{,}000/10^6 = 0.03\,\text{MT}\)
- \(Y = 1{,}500/10^6 = 0.0015\,\text{MT}\)
- \(W = 1.25I = \$2.50/\text{MT}\)
- \(R = 0.1I = \$0.20/\text{MT}\)

### With caching

Assume the first request writes the input to the cache, while the remaining 14 requests successfully reuse all cached
input tokens.

The first request costs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_1 &= X \cdot W + Y \cdot O \\
&= 0.03 \cdot 2.5 + 0.0015 \cdot 10 \\
&= 0.075 + 0.015 \\
&= \$0.09
\end{aligned}
$$

The remaining 14 requests cost:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{2-15} &= 14 (X \cdot R + Y \cdot O) \\
&= 14 (0.03 \cdot 0.2 + 0.0015 \cdot 10) \\
&= 14 (0.006 + 0.015) \\
&= 14 \cdot 0.021 \\
&= \$0.294
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the total cost with caching is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\text{cached}} &= C_1 + C_{2-15} \\
&= 0.09 + 0.294 \\
&= \boxed{\$0.384}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Without caching

Every request processes the input at the regular input price:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\text{request}} &= X \cdot I + Y \cdot O \\
&= 0.03 \cdot 2 + 0.0015 \cdot 10 \\
&= 0.06 + 0.015 \\
&= \$0.075
\end{aligned}
$$

For all 15 requests:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\text{uncached}} &= 15 (X \cdot I + Y \cdot O) \\
&= 15 (0.03 \cdot 2 + 0.0015 \cdot 10) \\
&= 15 \cdot 0.075 \\
&= \boxed{\$1.125}
\end{aligned}
$$

### How much do we save?

The absolute savings are:

$$
S = C_{\text{uncached}} - C_{\text{cached}}
= 1.125 - 0.384
= \$0.741
$$

The percentage reduction is:

$$
S_{\%} = \frac{S}{C_{\text{uncached}}} \cdot 100
\approx 65.9\%
$$

In this example, caching reduces the total cost by approximately **65.9%**.

## Final thoughts

Caching can significantly reduce costs when the same context is reused across multiple requests. However, cached content
is available only for a limited time, cache writes may cost more than regular input tokens, and only part of a prompt
may be eligible for caching.

Also, remember that a model's maximum context length is a limit, not the number of tokens automatically billed for every
request.

This is a simplified example, but the main idea is straightforward: **if you repeatedly send the same context, prompt
caching can save you money.**
