Business plan: Parallel rendering as a service

This proposal follows the intrapreneur guidelines. Legal, tax, and infrastructure costs are not included because the first version would be developed as a pilot inside an existing company.

Business opportunity

Many companies already have 3D models of products, buildings, machines, or environments, but turning those assets into high-quality images still requires powerful computers, specialized software, technical setup, and repeated manual work. This limits how quickly design, engineering, marketing, and sales teams can show realistic alternatives to customers.

The proposed business is a managed 3D visualization service: customers provide a standard glTF or GLB model and receive presentation-ready images from configured cameras, materials, and lighting. The service would use a parallel rendering pipeline to process several image tiles or jobs at once, making it possible to deliver multiple views and product variants without requiring the customer to own a powerful workstation.

Proposed solution

The existing C++ project is a strong technical foundation for this service. It already loads glTF and GLB scenes, supports cameras, textures, materials, shadows, direct lighting, indirect diffuse bounces, BVH acceleration, progressive tile rendering, and headless PNG output.

The customer would not need to configure a compiler, renderer, or virtual machine. A future web interface or API could accept a model, select a visual template, and return finished images with progress information and a rendering history.

Why not simply rent a cloud VM?

A technically skilled customer can rent a VM and run a renderer independently, so compute capacity alone is not a strong differentiator. The service would compete by removing the surrounding work: model validation, scene configuration, camera and lighting presets, batch generation, consistent output, storage, and integration with the customer's sales or content workflow.

This creates a more interesting product than raw infrastructure. A furniture company could upload one model and receive a complete set of catalog views and color variants. An architect could request the same building from several camera positions. A manufacturer could generate proposal images from a configured product before building a physical prototype. These repeatable workflows are difficult to maintain when every customer manages a separate VM.

Target customers and applications

  • Product and furniture companies that need catalog images and configurable variants.
  • Architectural and real-estate firms that need interior, building, and renovation previews.
  • Manufacturers that want sales images for industrial equipment before production.
  • Agencies and design studios that need an automated rendering backend without buying workstations.
  • Software companies that want an API for product configurators, digital twins, or proposal systems.

Value proposition

The service would shorten the path from an existing digital asset to a customer-facing visual. It could reduce manual rendering work, make design changes easier to compare, and let sales teams answer visual requests faster. Standard glTF/GLB input also makes the service compatible with many existing asset-production workflows instead of forcing customers into a proprietary scene format.

The strongest initial offer would be a managed rendering workflow, not an unrestricted general-purpose cloud renderer. Templates, validation, predictable output, and optional support would make the service easier to adopt and provide value that a bare VM does not.

Business model

  • Professional service: render supplied models and prepare reusable templates.
  • Subscription: provide a monthly number of jobs, stored projects, and standard output views.
  • API usage: charge by render time, image, resolution, or batch for integration with another product.
  • Enterprise deployment: install the service in private infrastructure when models are sensitive.

A small pilot should start with one narrow workflow, such as catalog images for configurable products. Pricing should be validated through customer interviews and a paid pilot rather than assumed in advance.

Competitive position and limitations

Commercial 3D packages offer more mature materials and artist tooling, while cloud VMs offer flexible compute. This project's opportunity is to provide a focused, programmable, and repeatable pipeline: open-standard scene input, native C++ performance, CPU portability, headless automation, and an architecture that can later use parallel workers or GPU acceleration.

It should not yet be marketed as a finished physically based renderer. Metallic and roughness values and normal maps are loaded, but the current shading model is mainly diffuse. The initial promise should focus on reliable automated visualization and workflow convenience, with rendering quality improving through the roadmap.

Development roadmap

  1. Validate the problem: interview product, architecture, manufacturing, and visualization teams and select one repeatable use case.
  2. Productize the prototype: add clear errors, structured logging, JSON render configuration, tests, benchmarks, camera and lighting presets, and documented input requirements.
  3. Improve visual quality: implement a complete physically based material model, normal mapping, environment lighting, tone mapping, and better sampling or denoising.
  4. Build the service: expose an API, add a job queue, progress reporting, project storage, access control, and an interface for uploading models and downloading results.
  5. Scale execution: parallelize tile and job processing, optimize memory use, and evaluate GPU or cloud worker acceleration.

Risks and mitigation

  • VM competition: focus on templates, automation, integration, and support rather than raw compute.
  • Rendering quality: begin with controlled use cases and disclose prototype limitations while improving materials and lighting.
  • Performance and cost: benchmark scenes early, use job limits, and add parallel workers only when demand justifies them.
  • Asset confidentiality: offer private deployment or isolated storage for industrial and commercial models.

Expected benefits

For an internal pilot, success would mean that a customer can submit a valid model, choose a template, and receive a consistent set of images without manually configuring a workstation. The main measurements should be time to first render, cost per completed image, successful-job rate, turnaround time for variants, and willingness to pay for the workflow.

Overall, the project is best presented as a functional rendering prototype with commercialization potential. Its business value is not simply access to a remote computer; it is the possibility of turning standard 3D assets into repeatable, automated visual content for products, spaces, and technical proposals.

Influence Strategies I Value Most

Those are

  1. Leading by Example: Demonstrating values and behaviors not only by words but also through actions. "A leader has to be in the midst of the troops" may summarize this concept. For example, when I teach tennis I usually highlight the fact that maintaining mental peace is really important particularly when things don't go as expected, as the opponent may take that as sign of weakness. I consider to do it myself, at least when playing tennis matches! 😊

  2. Inspirational Appeal: Appealing to shared values, collective awareness, and a common sense of purpose to motivate others. I consider this a healthy way to encourage cooperation and positive change. For example, when working in group and having a deadline soon: I usually encourage the members to prevent last minute crunching by rather destinating a bit of time working every day, as this will be healthier and logistically better for the all team.

I believe both strategies promote trust, authenticity, and mutual respect rather than relying on pressure or manipulation.

Change, how to surf the uncertainty?

One significant change in my life was deciding to leave Industrial Engineering after three years of classes and switch to Computer Science. I had a persistent feeling that something was wrong and that I needed to take a different path. I worried about the uncertainty of the future and felt sad about the possibility of losing three years of effort.

Four years later, I consider the change positive. Looking back, I realize that Industrial Engineering’s career opportunities did not particularly interest me. Computer Science, by contrast, gives me more opportunities to explore research, abstract ideas, and the theoretical side of problems, which I've allways been keen on. Furthermore, it's important to say that the first years of both degrees have a lot in common in terms of the topics involved, almost all of them were related to the same formal sciences. Thus, it wasn't much of a waste of time in that sense, maybe the opposite.

Several ideas from the course material can be explained by this process. First, the change involved resistance, especially the fear of the unknown and the desire for security. Second, Lewin’s model applies to my experience. The unfreezing stage began when I recognized my dissatisfaction with my original degree. The implementation stage involved changing careers and adapting to a new field. Finally, refreezing meant settling into the new course and confirming over time that it was a better fit.

The Stanley 1913 Company, their Mission, Vision and Values

Stanley 1913, founded by William Stanley Jr. in 1913, is a global lifestyle brand famous for inventing the all-steel vacuum bottle. For over a century, it has delivered durable, innovative food and beverage gear.

Vision: The company's vision is to "fuel the human experience, showing up for people who show up every day—across every continent, all around the world".

Mission: Stanley's mission is to "create, build and invent in a better way, for a better life and world". This is rooted in its "Built For Life™" promise, which drives its commitment to sustainability and reducing single-use waste.

Values: Stanley's core values are durability, relevance, and tradition —a commitment to products that last a lifetime, backed by a lifetime warranty. Parent company PMI adds guiding principles of sustainability, community, teamwork, accountability, and integrity.

👉 Source

Personal Opinion

What draws me to Stanley is its focus on quality and durability—values that feel increasingly overlooked today. In a culture where everything is treated as disposable, I am interested in products built to last, not to be replaced.

The company's logo is a crowned bear with wings. The bear represents resilience, the wings the nature or unbelievable, and the crown is a nod to the creator, who was a renown electrical engineer.

The Cultural Organization of Rockstar Games

Rockstar Games is one of the most influential video game developers in the world, renowned for creating the Grand Theft Auto (GTA) and Red Dead Redemption franchises. Founded in 1998 by Dan and Sam Houser, the company operates as a subsidiary of Take-Two Interactive and has grown to over 4,700 employees across 12 offices worldwide. Its games are known for their sprawling open worlds, cinematic storytelling, and immense cultural impact. The studio’s organizational culture is defined by a powerful tension between its relentless pursuit of creative excellence and a historically demanding work environment. At the heart of this is an almost obsessive commitment to secrecy—one that shapes every aspect of how the company operates, from how it hires and manages employees to the extreme security measures it deploys to protect its most valuable intellectual property.

Rockstar's approach to confidentiality is so extreme that it has drawn comparisons to Apple guarding the iPhone or Coca-Cola protecting its formula. In legal filings, the company itself has described its internal security culture as comparable to these industry giants, stating that "the standards and expectations regarding information security and privacy are exponentially beyond that of most employers". Every visitor to a Rockstar studio is required to sign a non-disclosure agreement (NDA), photography is prohibited, contractors are escorted by security personnel, and personal laptops, phones, and tablets are banned from connecting to Rockstar systems. The NDAs themselves are legendary in their severity. According to insider reports, Rockstar employees sign lifetime NDAs, meaning that developers cannot discuss specific details of their work even a decade after leaving the company. Topics such as the proprietary RAGE game engine are considered strictly taboo. A former technical director described the company as having "an almost pathological obsession with confidentiality". This secrecy is enforced by a dedicated team of around half-dozen investigators and a director whose sole job is to hunt for potential leaks. The company has taken extraordinary physical measures to prevent information from escaping. After individuals used drones to spy through office windows, Rockstar installed privacy film on its building windows. External storage devices are banned on office premises, and remote work has been heavily restricted—a controversial policy introduced after a massive 2022 data breach that saw early GTA 6 footage leaked online.

The gap between Rockstar's public narrative of creative excellence and the lived experiences of many employees reveals a culture caught between its artistic ambitions and the human cost of achieving them—a culture where silence is not just encouraged, but contractually enforced for life.
